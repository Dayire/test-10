01 · Anatomy

Mostly a tank, with engines bolted on

Cut a rocket open and there is almost nothing inside but two liquids. Oxidizer — liquid oxygen at −183 °C — sits on top; fuel — RP‑1, a refined kerosene — sits below. A jet engine breathes oxygen from the air. A rocket must carry its own, because the air runs out long before space does. That is the whole reason it is so big.

Everything else is thin structure: a first stage that does the heavy lifting, an interstage hiding the second stage's vacuum engine, and a fairing that shields the payload until the atmosphere is gone.

02 · Combustion

Turn two cold liquids into a 3,300 °C gas

Gravity alone can't feed an engine this hungry. A pair of turbopumps on one shaft spins at tens of thousands of rpm, driven by a small gas generator that burns a sliver of the propellant. They force kerosene and oxygen through the injector plate, where hundreds of tiny jets shatter the liquids into a fine mist that ignites on contact.

The chamber runs near 100 bar — a hundred times atmospheric pressure — at a temperature that would melt the walls in seconds. So the fuel is routed through channels in the wall first (the dashed amber lines), carrying the heat away before it is burned. This is regenerative cooling: the engine is cooled by its own fuel.

Mixture ratio: every kilogram of kerosene burns with about 2.3 kg of oxygen — which is why the oxygen tank is the bigger one.
03 · The nozzle

The nozzle is where heat becomes speed

Hot gas in the chamber is chaos — molecules bouncing in every direction. The nozzle turns that chaos into one direction. In the converging section the gas accelerates until it hits exactly Mach 1 at the throat, the narrowest point.

Then the counter‑intuitive part: past the throat the bell widens, and the gas gets faster, not slower. Supersonic flow speeds up as it expands, trading its pressure and heat for velocity. By the exit the exhaust is moving at nearly 3 km/s. The bright knots in the plume are shock diamonds — the jet re‑adjusting to the pressure of the air around it.

04 · Liftoff

Newton's third law, at 7.6 million newtons

A rocket doesn't push against the ground or the air. It pushes against its own exhaust. Nine engines hurl about 2.7 tonnes of gas every second downward at 2.8 km/s; the gas pushes back on the rocket with exactly the same force. That is the entire trick — it works just as well in vacuum.

It only rises when thrust beats weight. At 549 t the stack weighs 5.4 MN; full thrust is 7.6 MN. The surplus is small — it leaves the pad at about 0.4 g — and grows every second as propellant burns off.

Thrust equals weight at about 71 % throttle. Below it, the hold‑downs carry the difference. Above it, the rocket climbs.
05 · Staging

Why throw most of the rocket away?

Reaching orbit takes about 9.4 km/s of speed change — 7.8 to orbit plus what gravity and drag steal on the way up. The rocket equation says how much propellant that costs, and its answer is brutal: speed grows only with the logarithm of the mass ratio. Doubling the propellant does not double the speed.

Δv = ve · ln( m0 / mf )
m0 / mfmass ratio — full rocket divided by empty rocket veexhaust velocity — here ≈ 3.0 km/s

Empty tanks are dead weight you keep accelerating. Staging drops them, and the second stage starts fresh with a small, light rocket of its own — a second helping of the equation.

06 · Orbit

Orbit is falling — and missing

A rocket doesn't go up to orbit. It goes sideways. Seconds after liftoff it begins to lean, and most of its burn is spent gaining horizontal speed; altitude only matters for getting above the air. In the drawing the height is exaggerated — 400 km is barely 6 % of Earth's radius.

At 7.8 km/s something strange happens. The ship is still falling — gravity (grey arrow) pulls it toward Earth just as hard as ever. But it moves sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it at the same rate it falls. It falls forever, and never lands. That is an orbit.
